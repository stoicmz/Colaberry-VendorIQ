Foundations

The discipline, in the 20% an architect actually needs

Business analysis exists to close the gap between "what the business meant" and "what got built." An AI can draft requirements in seconds; it cannot know whether the draft is correct, complete, or safe to ship. That judgment is yours. This section is the minimum vocabulary and the review habits that let you exercise it.

Why BA exists

Every failed software project traces back to one of three gaps: the team built the wrong thing (a requirements gap), built the right thing wrong (a design gap), or built something nobody could safely operate (a non-functional gap). Business analysis is the discipline of closing the first and third gaps before money is spent on the second. An AI Solution Architect inherits that responsibility even when an AI drafts the paperwork â€” the model can hallucinate a plausible-looking requirement as easily as a true one, and a plausible-looking requirement that is wrong is more dangerous than an obviously bad one, because it doesn't get questioned.

The vocabulary â€” six things you must recognize on sight

Functional vs. non-functional

Functional requirements describe what the system does â€” "the system shall route a claim to the SIU queue when the fraud score exceeds 70." Non-functional requirements describe how well it does it, and are usually where AI-generated drafts fall short â€” latency, availability, security, auditability, accessibility. A requirements set with no non-functionals is a red flag, full stop.

User stories

A short, persona-anchored statement of value: As a [persona], I want [capability], so that [benefit]. The story is not the specification â€” the acceptance criteria attached to it are. A story without acceptance criteria is an idea, not a requirement.

Acceptance criteria (Given/When/Then)

The testable contract for a story. Given a starting state, When an action happens, Then an observable, checkable outcome follows. If a QA engineer can't write a test from it without asking you a follow-up question, it isn't done.

MoSCoW prioritization

Must have (the release fails without it), Should have (painful to omit, not fatal), Could have (nice, cut first under pressure), Won't have this time (explicitly descoped, so it stops resurfacing as scope creep). If everything in a backlog is "Must," nobody has actually prioritized anything â€” see the Architect's Review Lens below.

Personas

A synthesis of a real user type built from goals, context, and frustrations â€” deliberately not a demographic profile. "34-year-old, married, drives a sedan" tells you nothing about what a claimant needs from a claims system; "wants to avoid re-explaining the accident to three different people" tells you exactly what to build.

Use cases & traceability

A use case is the fuller narrative behind a story â€” main flow plus the alternate and exception paths that stories often skip. Traceability is the discipline of being able to answer, for any line of code, "which requirement made us build this, and which test proves it works" â€” see the RTM further down.

Good requirement vs. bad requirement

Bad â€” vague and untestable "The system should be fast and handle claims well, and should be secure."

No owner can say when this is met. No tester can write a test for it. It will be "done" the moment someone gets tired of arguing about it â€” which is exactly the failure mode BA exists to prevent.

Good â€” specific, measurable, bounded BR-N-002 â€” The system shall return an AI triage decision within 90 seconds (P95), measured from FNOL-submission timestamp to triage-result-written timestamp in the claims audit log.

Testable, owned, and it fails loudly in monitoring the moment it's violated â€” not six months later in a customer complaint.

KPIs â€” how you know the thing worked

A requirement tells the build team what to construct; a KPI tells the business whether constructing it was worth it. Every initiative you approve should carry 3â€“6 KPIs with a baseline, a target, and a measurement method fixed before build starts â€” otherwise "success" gets redefined after the fact to match whatever shipped. The Vision & Business Case document below shows a working example: cycle time, straight-through processing rate, leakage ratio, and CSAT, each with a number attached.

The architect's review lens

When an AI hands you a drafted requirement, story, or use case, run it through this checklist before you approve it:

CheckAsk thisReject ifâ€¦
TestableCould a QA engineer write a pass/fail test from this without asking me anything else?It uses words like "fast," "easy," "robust" with no number attached.
TraceableDoes this map to a business objective, and does something downstream (a story, a test) map back to it?It's an orphan â€” nothing justifies it, nothing verifies it.

The habit that matters mostAn AI-generated requirements set will look complete. Completeness is not correctness. Your value is catching the requirement that reads perfectly and is quietly wrong â€” the missing failure path, the KPI with no baseline, the "Must" that is actually a "Could." That is what "direct, evaluate, approve" means in practice.

UC-03 â€” SIU Fraud Signal Escalation

Primary actor: SIU Fraud Analyst. Secondary actor: ClaimsIQ AI Service. Trigger: A claim's fraud score exceeds the configured SIU referral threshold. Preconditions: Claim has completed triage (UC-02).

Main flow

2. Packet appears in the SIU queue within 2 hours of FNOL submission.
3. Analyst reviews the packet and opens a formal SIU investigation, or dismisses it.
4. If an investigation is opened, the claim is held from payment pending SIU clearance.

Alternate flow A â€” Analyst dismisses as false positive

3a. Analyst finds no genuine fraud indicators.
3a1. Analyst marks "Not fraud" with a reason category (see US-10); the claim returns to standard adjuster routing and the feedback is captured for the next model-tuning cycle, so the same false pattern doesn't keep firing.

1b. The packet-generation step errors (e.g., source transcript unreachable).
1b1. The referral still fires with the numeric score alone and an "Evidence Incomplete" flag â€” analysts are never left unaware of a high-risk claim merely because supporting evidence failed to render; a support ticket auto-opens to backfill the evidence.

UC-04 â€” Adjuster Review & Override of AI Triage

Primary actor: Claims Adjuster. Trigger: A claim requiring human review (any Complex or fraud-flagged claim, or any Low-Confidence claim from UC-02) appears in the adjuster's queue. Preconditions: Claim has an AI triage recommendation attached.

Main flow

1. Adjuster opens the claim and sees the AI's recommended tier, fraud score, and top 3 contributing factors.
2. Adjuster reviews the underlying claim data.
3. Adjuster accepts the recommendation as-is (in 3 clicks or fewer, BR-N-011), and the claim proceeds on that path.
